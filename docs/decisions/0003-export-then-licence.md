# 0003 — Export to two or three markets first, then licence

**Date:** August 2026 · **Status:** Accepted

## Context

The global route is either shipping product (export) or shipping the brand
(licensing). They have very different economics and require different companies.

| | Export | Licence |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Revenue per tin | ~R15.50 | ~R2.70 |
| Gross margin | ~38% | ~95% |
| Capital required | High | Almost none |

## Decision

**Both, sequenced.** Export to two or three markets from year 3, then licence
from year 4.

## Why

**You cannot licence a brand nobody has seen.** Every export market is a proof
point that raises the royalty rate and shortens the negotiation on the next
territory. Export is not an alternative to licensing — it is how licensing
becomes possible at a good price.

Benchmark food and beverage royalties run 4–6% of licensee net sales.

## Consequences

- Years 3–4 carry the working capital cost of export inventory, which licensing
  alone would have avoided.
- Trademark filings in target markets become urgent much earlier than trading
  does — including the six-month Paris Convention priority window at month 6.
- The brand book and product specification become deliverables with commercial
  value, not internal documents.
- **The US is likely to be a licence rather than an export**, given FDA
  requirements, distribution complexity, and the fact that the natural partner
  there is a creator with an audience rather than an importer.

## Revisit if

- A major US golf creator offers equity or a joint venture — take it and skip US
  export entirely.
- The rand moves past ~R20/USD, which materially improves export economics.
- A licensee offers a large minimum guarantee early — take it, but cap the term
  at three years.

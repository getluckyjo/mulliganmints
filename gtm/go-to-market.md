# Go-to-market

*How Mulligan Mints gets from a tin in a warehouse to a habit. Channel sizing is
in [`../research/03-channel-strategy-sa.md`](../research/03-channel-strategy-sa.md).*

---

## 1. The strategy in one paragraph

Win 300 golf clubs by hand. Use them to manufacture proof and content. Let the
content build an audience far larger than the South African market. Use the
audience to open bars, then retail, then export. Use export to prove the brand
travels. Then licence it.

**Every stage exists to make the next stage cheap.**

## 2. Positioning

> For golfers and the people who drink with them, Mulligan Mints is a
> genuinely strong mint in a tin worth keeping — the ceremonial second chance
> for the shot you'd rather forget. Unlike Wilson's XXX or Fisherman's Friend,
> which are mints you buy, Mulligan is a mint you *choose*, because it belongs
> to something.

The brand's competitive moat is not the formula. It is that the product has a
*place* — a sport, an occasion and a joke — and the incumbents' products do not.

## 3. Marketing model

Year 1 spend is **R380,000** — a fixed launch budget, not a percentage. From year
2 it runs at 14%, 13%, 12% and 11% of net revenue. Note it does not taper much:
the licensing thesis says the brand *is* the asset, so brand investment stays
high on purpose.

### Where the R380,000 goes

| | Approx | What it buys |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| **Golf club activation** | R120k | Club days, halfway house sampling, starter-box sampling, club championship stock, pro relationships |
| **Creator seeding** | R90k | SA golf creators first; then a small number of high-intent international seeds |
| **Content production** | R80k | The brand film, product photography, and a running stream of short-form |
| **Point of sale** | R50k | Counter units, display boxes, tee-box signage |
| **Launch event** | R60k *(in setup costs)* | One event at a host club. Press, creators, club pros. |
| **Trade & sampling stock** | R40k | Free stock, which is the cheapest marketing in this category |

### The single most important line: free stock

A tin costs us R10.29. A tin in the hand of a golfer who then tells three people in
his fourball is the cheapest customer acquisition available to us — better than
any paid impression, at any price. **Budget generously for giving product away in
year 1, and track it as marketing rather than as shrinkage.**

## 4. The creator strategy

This is the asymmetric bet in the plan, and it deserves stating plainly.

**The evidence:** Good Good Golf raised USD 45m in 2025 and does USD 40m+ in
revenue. Bob Does Sports has 1.1m subscribers and has launched both an apparel
line and an alcoholic drinks brand. Golf creators are no longer selling ad slots —
they are building consumer product businesses, and their audiences are buying.

**The opportunity:** a well-made mint tin is a natural fit for that ecosystem, and
nobody has done it. A tin is cheap to ship, photographs beautifully, is funny on
camera, and has an obvious on-course use. It is close to the ideal product for
golf content.

**The approach, in order:**

1. **South African creators first.** Cheaper, closer, and they can be at the
   launch. Build the playbook here.
2. **Seed internationally, do not buy.** Send tins. No brief, no contract, no
   expectation. A tin that costs us R10.29 landed in the hands of a US golf creator
   is the cheapest shot on goal in this business. Send hundreds.
3. **Convert seeding into partnership, not sponsorship.** Do not try to buy a
   sponsorship from Good Good or Bob Does Sports — we cannot afford it and it
   would not work. Aim for a *collaboration*: a co-branded flavour, a limited tin,
   a revenue share. These businesses now launch products; the pitch is
   "launch one with us", not "please post about us".
4. **A creator relationship may become the licensing deal.** The most likely
   route into the US is not an importer. It is a partner with an audience. Treat
   every seeded tin as a licensing lead.

**The risk, stated honestly:** any of these channels could launch a mint
themselves, with an audience we would need five years and R20m to build. That is
a real competitive threat and it argues for speed and for approaching them as
partners early rather than competing quietly.

## 5. Owned channels

**Instagram and TikTok** — the brand's home. Product, tins, course content, the
joke. High frequency, low production cost.

**YouTube** — long-form is where golf culture actually lives. Do not try to build
a channel from scratch. Show up in other people's.

**The webshop** — small in volume, our highest margin (R35/tin net), and the only
place we own the customer. Also the artefact that makes a retail buyer take the
meeting.

**Email** — every DTC customer, every club pro, every corporate golf day
organiser. Unglamorous and it compounds.

## 6. Sales model by phase

| Phase | Who sells | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Y1 | Founder + 1 rep | Physically drive to clubs. Sample, place a display, come back in three weeks. |
| Y2 | 2–3 reps | Same, plus the first distributor for bars, plus the first regional retail pilot |
| Y3 | Sales manager + 3 reps + distributors | Golf stays direct forever. Bars move to distributors. Retail managed centrally. |
| Y4–5 | + export manager | Add importers by market. Golf still direct. |

**Golf clubs are never handed to a distributor.** Three hundred outlets, the whole
brand story, and the content engine. It is worth the cost to serve.

## 7. What "proof of concept" actually means

The pilot is not "did we sell some mints". It has to produce four specific,
defensible numbers, because those numbers are what the growth round is raised on
and what a licensee eventually buys:

1. **Tins per outlet per month**, by channel, measured over at least 90 days
2. **Reorder rate** — what share of outlets place a second order, and how fast
3. **Sell-through by placement** — pro shop counter vs halfway house vs bar
4. **Cost of acquiring an outlet** — rep hours and free stock per new door

Everything else is a story. These four are evidence.

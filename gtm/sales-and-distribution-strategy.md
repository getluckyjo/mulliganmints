# Sales and distribution strategy

*For the dataroom. How Mulligan Mints physically gets from a container in Durban
onto a pro shop counter, and who we have to convince along the way.*

---

## The question we set out to answer

**Do South African golf clubs order through liquor distributors?**

**No — and that matters, because it is the obvious assumption and it is wrong.**

South African liquor distribution is beverage-only and vertically controlled.
Heineken Beverages (Distell, Namibia Breweries and Heineken SA combined in 2023),
AB InBev/SAB, Diageo and Pernod Ricard run their own routes; redistributors like
Super Discount Liquors and Oasis carry those same principals' lines. **Their reps
sell beer, wine and spirits.** A confectionery SKU is not their category, not
their margin, and not what their rep is incentivised on. A mint does not travel
on a beer truck.

Confectionery reaches on-consumption venues through **FMCG distributors** —
Key Distributors (Nestlé, Unilever, Tiger Brands, Reckitt, GSK portfolios; depots
in Cape Town, Johannesburg, Durban, Nelspruit, Polokwane and Bloemfontein, on
48-hour delivery) and Maspark (snacks, confectionery and beverages from a Cape
Town facility into retailers, wholesalers, forecourts and independents) — or
through **cash and carry**, where a great many venues simply send someone to
Makro or a Massmart CBW/Jumbo/Trident branch each week.

## The finding that shapes everything: a golf club is not one customer

This is the single most important thing in this document.

**At most South African clubs the pro shop is not run by the club.** It is leased
or franchised:

- **Clovelly Golf Club** — pro shop operated by **Global Golf**, a national company
- **Wanderers Golf Club** — a PGA of South Africa affiliated shop run by
  professionals
- **Port Shepstone Country Club** — run by the resident PGA professional, who is
  also the course superintendent

So one golf club can contain **three separate buying decisions**:

| Who | Controls | What they care about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The pro shop operator** — often a PGA pro or a company like Global Golf, trading on their own account | The counter. The highest-value placement we have. | Margin per square centimetre of counter, and cash tied up in stock |
| **The F&B or bar operator** — frequently a contracted caterer, not the club | The bar, the halfway house, the beverage cart | Speed of sale, no waste, simple reordering |
| **The club manager / GM** | Corporate golf days, member gifts, club championship stock | Member experience, sponsorship, doing the club a favour |

**"Sign a golf club" is not one sale.** The pitch, the price and the person are
different for each. A rep who walks into the clubhouse and asks for "the person
who buys the sweets" gets three shrugs.

**Practical consequence:** the first question on every call is *"who owns the pro
shop — the club or you?"* The answer determines everything that follows.

**This has to be briefed to the distributor's reps.** They carry hundreds of
lines and will not work it out alone — but it is the difference between selling
one account at a club and selling three.

## Our model: distributor-led from day 1

[Decision 0009](../docs/decisions/0009-distributor-led.md) settled this. **A
distributor with an existing sales team covers golf clubs and bars from launch.**
We sell to them at **R20.00 a tin**; they sell to the venue at R24.25, which
retails at R45 — an **18% distributor margin**. DTC stays ours at R35.

### Why — the coverage arithmetic that forced it

A field rep manages **30–40 calls a week**. Here is what a direct plan would have
demanded, on a four-week call cycle:

| Year | Outlets | Field heads | Calls/week each |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 100 | 2 | 13 |
| 2 | 240 | 2 | 30 |
| **3** | **420** | **2** | **53** ⚠ |
| 5 | 750 | 4 | 47 ⚠ |

**From year 3 a direct plan asks for more calls than people can physically
make**, and hiring does not fix it — even four field heads in year 5 sit at 47 a
week. That arithmetic is why the plan is distributor-led, and it is worth showing
an investor: the decision was forced by a number, not by preference.

The distributor's team also opens outlets far faster than two founders could —
**120 golf clubs and 150 bars in year 1**, against 60 and 40 selling direct, and
300 clubs and 1,800 bars by year 5.

### What comes back with it

**They warehouse the stock.** We take delivery of a container and move it once,
to one address. They hold it, pick it and deliver to every outlet. That removes
the entire 3PL layer — no storage rental, no pick-and-pack, no outbound courier
to hundreds of venues. **Logistics fall from R1.35 a tin to R0.20.**

**One invoiced customer instead of three hundred.** No credit control across
hundreds of small venues, no route accounting or field-sales software, no
per-outlet invoicing, and far less travel without a field force. Overheads run at
**30% of a conventional base rather than 40%.**

Together those are worth roughly **R790,000 of cumulative EBITDA** across five
years and take break-even from 4,500 tins a month down to **3,750**.

### What it costs

**R4.25 a tin.** On the year-5 plan that is roughly R2.7m of gross profit handed
over. We buy back reach we could not otherwise have had, and a cost base with no
field force or warehouse.

**And a concentrated debtor book.** One customer at 45 days is now most of the
receivables. If they do not pay, there is nothing else to collect. Credit-check
them before signing, and consider credit insurance on the balance — the admin
saving is real, and so is the concentration that creates it.

**R20.00 is a thin cut at 18%**, against the ~26% a distributor would normally
want for a single-SKU startup brand. It is achievable because **Get Lucky's
access means we hand them warm outlets rather than a cold prospecting list.**
That is the reason the terms are good, and it should be said plainly in the
negotiation.

### The two things to contract for

**1. Sell-through data by outlet, by month.** The four numbers this business
exists to produce now sit in the distributor's system: tins per outlet per month,
reorder rate, sell-through by placement, cost to acquire an outlet. **A global
licensee will want that evidence, and "our distributor has it" is not an answer.**
Write the reporting obligation into the agreement.

**2. Minimum performance.** Outlet counts by channel, call frequency, and an
annual review against agreed targets. A distributor carrying hundreds of lines
prioritises what sells; our job is to define what trying looks like before we
find out they weren't.

### The dependency this creates

One partner now holds every outlet except DTC. If they deprioritise us or the
relationship ends, there is no field force to fall back on.

- **Keep the Get Lucky relationships warm** at the 30 courses. Golf is only 300
  outlets and the founders are already there — it could be taken back in-house.
- **Review annually.** Distributor margins compress as a brand builds pull. The
  18% is a starting point, not a settlement.
- **Agree in writing what happens to stock in their warehouse if the agreement
  ends.** They hold our inventory. That needs to be addressed on the way in, not
  discovered on the way out.

Tracked as R-17 in `ops/risk-register.md`.

### What stays ours

**DTC at R35 a tin.** Small in volume, highest margin, and the only place we own
the customer and the data outright.

**Corporate golf days and gifting** at R28–32 for 500+ tins. Paid up front,
near-zero cost to serve, and they come through Get Lucky's own event calendar
rather than the distributor's routes.

## Trade terms by route

| Route | Our net per tin | Their margin | Terms |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| **Distributor — golf and bars** | **R20.00** | ~18% to them | 45 days |
| Corporate / gifting, 500+ tins | R28–32 | n/a | Paid up front |
| DTC | R35.00 | n/a | Paid up front |

The distributor sells on to the venue at R24.25; the venue retails at R45 for a
38% margin. **We hold one trade price** — and that discipline applies to the
distributor's own list as much as to ours.

**The unit that gets sold is the display box, not the tin.** Twelve tins for
R291 ex-VAT, retailing at R540 incl VAT — **R178.57 of margin, 38%**. Lead with
that.

## How we actually win a club

1. **Establish who owns the pro shop.** Club or concession. Everything follows.
2. **Sample first, sell second.** Hand over tins. The product does the work —
   it is genuinely strong and people react to it.
3. **Place one display box on the counter.** Not a shelf, not a basket. The
   counter, next to the till, where people pay for their round.
4. **Free sampling stock for the halfway house and the starter.** This is
   marketing spend, not shrinkage, and it should be budgeted as such.
5. **Return in three weeks and count what is left.** The reorder is the entire
   test. A club that reorders has proved the product sells.
6. **Then ask for the corporate golf day.** Once the counter is working, the
   goodie-bag order is a much easier conversation — and it is the most profitable
   order in the business.

## What we still do not know

Stated plainly, because a dataroom that hides its gaps is worse than one that
names them:

1. **What share of SA clubs lease their pro shop versus running it themselves.**
   We have three worked examples, not a survey. This changes who we call and how
   the pitch is framed, and thirty phone calls would answer it.
2. **Whether any distributor treats golf clubs as a defined channel.** We found
   none. If one exists, it would be a significant shortcut.
3. **Whether golf club groups buy centrally.** Sun International runs five
   courses; if group-level buying exists anywhere, one conversation replaces
   fifty.
4. **What a pro shop's actual counter sell-through is** for any impulse item.
   No benchmark exists. The 90-day pilot is the only way to find out.

**Items 1 to 3 are answerable this month, by telephone, for nothing.** They
should be closed before the dataroom goes out.

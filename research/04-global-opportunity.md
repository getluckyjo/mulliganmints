# The global route — licence or export?

*The decision that determines what kind of company this is. Sources in [`sources.md`](sources.md).*

> **Decided: licensing only.** [Decision 0008](../docs/decisions/0008-bootstrap-route.md)
> adopted the bootstrap route, which has no capital for export inventory. The
> recommendation below — export first, then licence — was the right answer for a
> funded plan and is **superseded**. We licence from year 3 without exporting
> first, and the substitute proof point is South African sell-through data rather
> than an export track record. Everything else here still stands: what a licensee
> is buying, the target territories, the royalty benchmarks, and the Irish-origin
> constraint.

---

## 1. The question

South Africa is the proving ground, not the prize. 139,496 registered golfers and
4.2 million rounds a year is a market big enough to prove sell-through and small
enough to prove it cheaply. It is not big enough to be the business.

The real question is what happens after: **do we ship mints, or do we ship the
brand?**

## 2. The two models, honestly compared

| | **Export** | **Licence** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What we sell | Tins, FOB South Africa | The right to make and sell under our brand |
| Revenue per tin | ~R15.50 | ~R2.70 (6% of licensee net sales) |
| Gross margin | ~38% | ~95% |
| Capital required | High — stock, freight, credit risk in every market | Almost none |
| Operational load | Heavy: freight, duties, registrations, importer management | Light: brand governance, quality audit, royalty collection |
| Speed to a new market | 6–12 months | 3–6 months, and the licensee does the work |
| Control | Total | Contractual only |
| Downside | We carry every unit of inventory risk | A bad licensee damages the asset and we cannot easily take it back |
| What has to be true | Our cost base has to beat a local manufacturer's, landed | The brand has to be worth more than the product |

**Benchmark royalty rates for food and beverage brands run 4–6% of licensee net
sales** (RoyaltyRange), which is what the model uses at 6%.

## 3. The recommendation

**Both, sequenced — and the sequence is the strategy.**

> **Export first, to two or three markets, precisely so that licensing is
> possible later.** You cannot licence a brand nobody has seen. Every export
> market is a proof point that raises the royalty rate and shortens the
> negotiation on the next one.

Modelled accordingly:

| | Y3 | Y4 | Y5 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Export units | 90,000 | 320,000 | 650,000 |
| Export revenue | R1.54m | R5.74m | R12.25m |
| Licensing revenue | — | R0.40m | R1.80m |

Licensing starts in year 4 with a single territory and R400k of signing fees —
no royalty flow yet, because the licensee needs a year to get to market. By year
5 it is three territories: R900k of signing fees plus 6% on R15m of licensee net
sales.

**Look at what that R1.8m costs us to earn: almost nothing.** It carries roughly
95% margin and consumes no working capital. It is 4% of year-5 revenue and a
much larger share of year-5 profit. The licensing line is the reason the plan
reaches a 31% EBITDA margin, and it is the reason brand investment stays at 11%
of revenue in year 5 instead of tapering. **The brand is the asset being built.
The mints are how we prove it is worth something.**

## 4. Where to go, in what order

### Tier 1 — first export markets

**United Kingdom and Ireland.** The obvious first move, and the awkward one.
Fisherman's Friend's home market, a mature strong-mint audience, and enormous
golf participation. The Irish angle is either our best story here or our biggest
liability — see §6.

**United States.** The real prize, and the one the whole golf-creator thesis
points at. Enormous golf market, a proven appetite for creator-adjacent consumer
brands, and Altoids has left the premium-tin position badly under-served since
Mars took it mainstream. Hardest to enter (FDA labelling, state distribution,
brutal competition) and the most likely to be a **licence rather than an export**
for exactly that reason.

**Australia.** Under-considered and well-suited. Strong golf culture, similar
retail structure to SA, a comparable pub trade, and no local incumbent in premium
strong mints.

### Tier 2 — opportunistic

UAE and Gulf (golf tourism, high-spend, tin formats do well as gifts); Kenya and
Nigeria (SADC/African expansion where SA brands travel with credibility);
Netherlands and Germany (huge strong-mint consumption, mature licensing markets).

## 5. What a licensee is actually buying

Worth being clear about this before the first conversation, because it determines
what we spend the next three years building:

1. **A trademark portfolio**, registered in their territory before they ask.
2. **A brand book** — the identity, tone, and rules PJ Offner is being
   commissioned to build.
3. **A proven product specification** — formula, tin, supplier, tested and
   costed.
4. **Evidence of sell-through** — the SA numbers, by channel, by month.
5. **An audience** — social following, creator relationships, earned media.

Items 1, 2 and 3 are cheap and inside our control. Items 4 and 5 are what the
South African pilot exists to produce. **Everything in this plan before year 4 is
the manufacture of a licensable asset.**

## 6. The Irish question — a real risk, worth getting right

The design reference is Irish: green, clover, heritage tin. "Mulligan" is an
Irish surname, and the most-repeated origin story for the golf term is
Irish-Canadian. It is a good story and it fits the brand.

**It is also a claim we cannot make.** The product is manufactured in China and
the company is South African. Under South African labelling law (R146) a label
must not mislead as to origin, and EU and UK rules are stricter still. If the
brand reads as an *Irish product*, we have a compliance problem in exactly the
markets we most want to license into — and a licensee's legal team will find it
in the first review.

**The fix is cheap and does not cost us the story: build the Irish reference as
character and homage, never as provenance.** Mulligan is a person, a legend, a
patron saint of the second chance. He is not a factory in County Cork. Concretely:

- No "Product of Ireland", no "Irish recipe", no shamrock-plus-Gaelic-lettering
  combination that reads as a country-of-origin mark.
- Country of origin stated plainly and correctly on the label.
- The heritage story told in the *language of legend*, in a register that is
  obviously mythology rather than provenance.

Briefed to PJ Offner in `brand/brand-brief-pj-offner.md` and carried in
`ops/risk-register.md` as R-06.

## 7. What would change the recommendation

- **If a major US golf creator wants equity or a joint venture** — take it, and
  skip export to the US entirely. That relationship is worth more than the margin.
- **If SA sell-through comes in above plan** — slow the export push and take
  more of the domestic market first. Domestic margin is better and the proof is
  cheaper.
- **If the rand collapses past ~R20/USD** — export economics improve sharply and
  the case for shipping product rather than licensing gets much stronger.
- **If a licensee offers a large minimum guarantee early** — take it, but cap the
  term at three years. Do not sell a ten-year licence on a two-year-old brand.
